Ladders and Floors Proof of concept

Proof of concept. The companies, events and dates are real and sourced. The stick figures are fictional workers whose careers are simulated: they illustrate what typically happened to people like them, not what happened to any real person. Don't use it for real career or investment decisions.

Vibe-coded. Designed and directed by Zolyx, and built with Claude (Anthropic) in a single conversation, without writing code by hand. Sources are cited throughout, but the model hasn't been reviewed by a labour economist.

Each ladder is a real company. Every company has all six career stages, from entry level to executive, but not the same number of seats on each: rung spacing shows how hard each step is there (dense rungs, many seats above; sparse rungs, few). Width is company size. Colour shows, year by year, the prospects of the group of jobs the company stands for (its industry and size): job openings, layoff risk and growth, from BLS and company data. Those same numbers drive the simulated workers there, so a red ladder really is a riskier place to be. The companies are a sample chosen to match the real US economy by industry and company size. The stick figures are simulated workers who climb, switch companies, start over, or lose their job and look for another.

      How each crisis shifted this run

      Each event is compared with the years just before it, in this same simulation, next to the real US change for the same comparison. The workers are simulated, so exact numbers change on every restart; the direction of the shift is what to look at. Figures are per year, so periods of different length compare fairly. They fill in as the run reaches each period.

      Model vs real world

      Every number the model can be checked on, measured live in this run and set against the real US figure for the same years. Match is how close the run comes, from 0 to 100%: for a number, 100% minus the gap as a share of the real value; for a mix of jobs, the share of workers in the right place; for a range, 100% anywhere inside it. The overall score is the plain average. Typical is the average over several full runs. Some metrics can only be measured once the run reaches their year.

      What the data says

      Survivors outgrow their industry

      Of 114 US companies whose yearly headcount was checked against their industry, almost none followed it on its own: companies that survive and stay listed usually grew 2–30% a year faster. Groups of companies chosen together do follow it: every industry's group stays within about 11% of its real employment, 2000–2025.

      Construction is the least stable industry

      In the real data, construction lays off about 37 of every 100 workers a year, against about 6 in government (BLS JOLTS, average 2001–2025). Leisure and hospitality has the most quits: about 42 per 100 workers a year.

      2008 in the data

      Quits fell about a third, layoffs rose about a fifth and the typical job search more than doubled (BLS JOLTS and CPS). People who had a job held on to it.

      People stayed put after the crisis

      Median time with the current employer rose from 3.5 years in 2000 to 4.6 years in 2012–14, then fell back to 3.9 years by 2024 (BLS Employee Tenure surveys).

      The jobs moved

      Manufacturing held about 13% of US jobs in 2000 and about 8% today; education and health grew from about 12% to 18% (BLS Current Employment Statistics).

      What's real and what's simulated

      Real, the same every time

      The companies, their headcounts year by year, their industries and founding years. Every rate that moves over time: quits and layoffs month by month in each industry, the mix of jobs by industry and company size, job openings, and the dates of 9/11, the 2008 crisis, COVID-19 and the Great Resignation, which are shown as markers.

      Simulated, different on every restart

      The workers. No record exists of every employee's career, so the model creates fictional ones: a statistical sample (2,400 workers at Standard detail; 1,600 at Light, 3,200 at Full) that behaves like the real US workforce and produces every number on this page, and about 80 visible stick figures (50 at Light, 105 at Full) with six personas (climbers, job hoppers, nepo hires and so on) that tell individual stories but are not counted in the numbers. The rules are tuned to the real figures where data exists. Each simulated year it rolls the dice for each one: promoted or not, quit or not, laid off or not, which company hires them. This is an agent-based simulation.

      What that means

      The model illustrates rather than predicts. It doesn't say "this person at Ford lost their job". It says "a typical worker in a group of jobs like this one would have faced something like this". Pressing Restart replays the same history with a fresh roll of the dice, so individual stories change while the patterns stay. A single run can be skewed by luck; an effect that shows up on every restart is a real effect of the rules.

      Does the sample mirror its industries?

      Each industry is represented by a group of real US companies chosen because, together, their own headcounts rose and fell like the industry's real employment from 2000 to 2025 (BLS Current Employment Statistics). Hardly any single company does that: the survivors that stay listed usually grow much faster than their industry. So the groups mix fast and slow growers, and different kinds of business, so that together they form a scaled-down version of the industry. Headcounts come from annual reports via Stock Analysis and Macrotrends. Solid line: the industry's real jobs; dashed: the group's average headcount; both start at 100 in 2000. Groups whose companies only report from 2009 show the earlier years with fewer companies. Firms under 1,000 staff rarely publish long histories, so the small stand-ins are not tested; they follow their size group's data.

      The companies in the model

      Every company in the model hires simulated workers and counts in every chart and score. Each industry is represented by its mirror group (the companies whose headcounts together follow the industry), by small firms that stand for its jobs in firms under 1,000 staff, where about half of US private-sector jobs are, and, for government, by public employers. Companies were added only where they fit this; none were added for being well known. The Detail setting changes how many companies represent each industry: Light keeps the core of each group (about 72 companies), Standard about 98, Full about 124. Each level was checked on its own, so every group mirrors its industry at every setting; the charts below show the level in use. The visible stick figures, with their six personas, are not counted: the numbers come from the statistical sample.

      How to read it, and what it can't tell you

      Every company has every career stage. Every ladder starts on the ground floor, L0 Entry level, and reaches L5 Executive, because every company, however small, has someone in charge: in a 40-person firm that is the owner or general manager, who the BLS counts as a top executive. What differs is how many seats each floor has, so the same step can be easy at one company and very hard at another. Rung spacing shows this: dense rungs below a floor mean many seats on it, sparse rungs mean few. A ladder's width shows the company's size. Until version 25, height showed company size and small companies stopped at Junior; once the sample matched real company sizes, that rule left only 0.2% of workers able to reach Executive, against 2.4% in reality.

      Every floor has a limited number of seats. A company can only have so many directors and executives, so nobody, nepo hires included, can move onto a floor unless a seat there is free. Across the whole economy, seats follow the proportions of real US jobs from the BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (May 2025): top executives and general managers about 2.4% of all jobs, other managers 4.8%, supervisors and team leads about 5% (our estimate), professional roles 25.8%, and the rest split evenly between junior and entry-level work. Each company's own mix differs in three ways. Industry: Professional seats follow the industry's share of professional and business specialist jobs (BLS Current Population Survey, September 2026), and the management floors its share of managers (BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, May 2023): 58% of jobs in education and health are professional, against 4% in construction, so becoming Professional is far easier in a hospital than on a building site; managers are 13.6% of jobs in finance but 3.9% in transportation. Size: bigger firms have more layers of management (French employer data, Caliendo, Monte and Rossi-Hansberg 2015), so a small firm has an owner but almost no directors, and a large one many directors; the exact factors are our assumption. All companies are then calibrated together so the economy as a whole keeps the BLS proportions. When a seat opens, someone from the floor below usually fills it within about a year, as one departure sets off a chain of promotions; climbers push about 1.5 times as hard for it, and nepo hires 2.6 times as hard where their family controls the company (in the sample, families control Ford, Tyson Foods, Comcast, The New York Times, Dillard's, Marcus Corp and Scholastic through special voting shares) but only 1.4 times elsewhere. Overall, the simulated workforce stays close to the real pyramid: about 33% entry level, 30% junior, 25% senior, 5% leads, 4% directors and 2% executives, against 31, 31, 26, 5, 4.8 and 2.4%.

      Staying in the same field. About 37 to 39% of US job-to-job moves stay in the same industry sector (Census Bureau Job-to-Job Flows, as analysed by the Richmond Fed for 2019 to 2022), and the model keeps a similar share. Every industry is now represented by several companies of different sizes, so a worker can stay in their field without returning to the employer they left. Staying in the field also follows the same rebalancing as hiring, so each industry keeps close to its real share of jobs. Workers passed over for promotion usually look for a better-rated company in their own industry. Career changers always start over in a different industry from the one they left.

      Colour is the group's prospects, year by year. Each company stands for a group of jobs (its industry and size), so its ladder shows how that group was doing at that moment: how many jobs were open in its industry, how often people there were laid off (adjusted for company size), and how fast jobs grew, its own headcount where the annual reports give it. All three come from BLS data and company reports, month by month from 2001 to 2026, and they are the same numbers that drive the simulated workers on that ladder. In 40-run tests, workers on the reddest fifth of ladder-years were laid off about 2.4 times as often as on the greenest fifth.

      The simulation is a set of rules fed by data. A worker may be promoted when a seat on the floor above is free at their company, which happens when someone above leaves (at the real rates of their industry and floor) or the company grows. Seats are usually filled from the floor below within about a year. Ladders appear in the year the company was founded. Where these numbers are not calibrated to data, they are illustrative assumptions chosen to make the dynamics visible.

      Growing ladders. Ladders widen and narrow as companies grow and shrink, following each company's real yearly headcount from its annual reports: Ford falls from about 346,000 staff in 2000 to about 169,000, HCA Healthcare grows, the US Postal Service shrinks from about 925,000 to 624,000. Companies founded after 2000, such as Duolingo and United Homes Group, start narrow in their founding year and widen to today's size, with estimated timing. A company's size group can change as it grows or shrinks, and with it the group of jobs it stands for.

      US workforces. Because the model's economy is the United States, each company hires in proportion to its US workforce and is placed in the size group of that workforce, as the Census counts firm size: Ford has about half of its staff in the US, Tyson about 90%. Some US shares are our estimates. Job cuts at a company are not added as separate events: they are already in its yearly headcount and in its industry's JOLTS layoff rate.

      The events are markers only. 9/11, the 2008 financial crisis, COVID-19 and the Great Resignation are shown as shaded bands and banners, but they change nothing in the model: no rate, ratio or share depends on them, and switching them off changes only what is displayed. Everything that moves over time comes from real dated data: quits and layoffs from JOLTS month by month, job-search length from the yearly median duration of unemployment, the industry mix by year, layoffs by floor from occupation data by year. So the events show what the numbers did at those times: after 9/11, quits fell about 12% while layoffs barely moved; in 2008–10, quits fell about a third, layoffs rose about a fifth and job searches more than doubled; in March–April 2020 layoffs jumped about sixfold, unevenly across industries (in the worst month restaurants, hotels and entertainment laid off about 28% of their staff, government about 1%; JOLTS, BLS Monthly Labor Review, June 2021). New events can be added as markers without touching any number. Layoffs above the normal level hit workers broadly rather than mainly the frequent movers, as recession layoffs reach settled workers too, and every quit and layoff is drawn ahead using each month's real rate and placed on the date it happens.

      Turnover over the years. Quits and layoffs follow the BLS JOLTS survey month by month from January 2001 to August 2026, industry by industry: each of the ten industries uses its own monthly rates, seasonal swings included (winter layoffs in construction, the January drop in transport, the school year in government), so construction's 2008–10 collapse, the 2010 census jobs in government and leisure's 32% layoff rate in March 2020 all happen in their own industry. Quits ran at about 26 per 100 workers a year in 2001, fell to 16 in 2009, climbed to 28 in 2019 and peaked at about 33 in 2021–22. The economy-wide rates are not set directly; they come out of the industries, as in reality, within a few percent of JOLTS each year. A move straight to another employer counts as a quit, as it does in JOLTS. The mix of jobs changes every year too, from BLS employment by industry (July of each year): manufacturing held about 13% of US jobs in 2000 and about 8% today, while education and health grew from about 12% to 18%.

      Industry mix and hiring. Simulated workers are hired so that each industry gets its real share of US jobs in that year (Bureau of Labor Statistics); today that is education and health 18%, government 15%, leisure and hospitality 14%, professional and business services 14%, trade 14%, manufacturing 8%, finance 6%, construction 6%, transportation 5%, and information, where most tech companies sit, under 2%. Inside an industry, jobs are also split by company size in real proportions (see below), each company sharing its size group's jobs with the others in it in proportion to its size, So a tech company can appear in the model as a ladder without taking a bigger share of the workforce than tech really has. Companies' sizes change over time where their growth is modelled.

      Is the sample representative? A handful of companies can't contain the US economy, so each company stands for a group of jobs: its industry and its size group. Real US jobs are split over those groups and each group's jobs are shared by the companies in it. A 38-person manufacturer like Electro-Sensors therefore stands for all manufacturing jobs in firms under 100 staff (about 2.4% of US jobs), and Kroger can take no more than its share of retail jobs in the largest firms. Every industry has companies in at least four of the five size groups. Finance, information, professional services and construction have no company with 100,000 or more US staff whose history follows its industry (the biggest banks, for example, grew mostly by buying other banks), so those jobs go to the industry's companies in the next size group down. Industry shares come from the BLS (September 2026). Company sizes come from Census and BLS firm data: firms under 100 staff hold about 34% of private-sector jobs (BLS Business Employment Dynamics, Q1 2025: 34.1%), firms under 500 about 46% (Census Statistics of U.S. Businesses, 2021: 45.9%), firms with 10,000 or more about 30% (Census, 2019). The split between 10,000–99,999 and 100,000 or more (21% and 9%) and the share of firms with 500–999 staff are our estimates. Each industry's share in small firms also comes from Census data: 82% of construction jobs are in firms under 500 staff, against 28% in finance. Government is matched by level instead (federal 11%, state 23%, local 65%; BLS, September 2026), because there is no national data on the size of public employers. Headcounts of the companies added for this come from their annual reports (10-K) or official figures. In a full run the simulated workforce stays within about 2 points of every industry's real share and within about 2 points of every size group's.

      How the companies were chosen. Each industry is represented by companies whose own headcount histories, together, rise and fall like the industry's real employment from 2000 to 2025 (see the charts above). Within an industry they cover different size groups and different kinds of business, so careers there vary the way they do in the real industry. Firms with fewer than 1,000 US staff rarely publish long histories; they stay as stand-ins for their size group, whose jobs and turnover follow size-class data. Companies that grew or shrank very differently from their industry, or work mostly outside the US, stay on the map, faded, but hire no simulated workers.

      Age, retirement and a settled start. Every simulated worker has an age. Newcomers start between 18 and 26, and people retire at about 62 on average, rarely before 55 or after 70 (Gallup's average retirement age is 61–62). Young workers change jobs far more often than older ones, following the US National Longitudinal Survey of Youth (NLSY79): about 0.8 new jobs a year at 18–24, falling to about 0.2 at 45 and over. Retirees are replaced by newcomers at entry level, as in a real workforce. Before the run starts in 2000, the statistical sample quietly lives through 20 years of job changes so it begins in a realistic, settled state; then every worker is given an age from the real age mix of employed Americans in 2000 (median about 39), older workers more often on higher floors. As the baby boomers age, the median rises to about 42 by the mid-2020s, as it did in reality. Promotions also come mostly early in a career (Pergamit and Veum; McCue), so the median age on each floor ends up close to BLS occupation data: about 33 at entry level, 46 for managers and 52 at the top (chief executives: 51.6).

      Movers and stayers. A small group of workers changes jobs again and again, while most stay put for years. Each simulated worker has a lasting tendency to move, drawn at random; layoffs are even more concentrated on the frequent movers than quits, because most layoffs and discharges counted by JOLTS are recalls, dismissals and the end of short, seasonal or temporary jobs. Only a few per 100 workers a year lose a job they had settled into: the Displaced Workers Survey finds 7–10% of workers over three normal years and about 16% in 2007–09 (Farber). The gap between movers and stayers narrows with age. The result matches the BLS tenure survey (January 2024): a median of about 4 years with the current employer and about a quarter of workers with 10 years or more. These factors only decide who is let go within a floor and an industry; how often each floor and each industry is hit comes from the data, so no industry's layoff rate drifts from its BLS level. In career stories, a company with fewer than 100 staff is named as "a small firm like …", because it stands for many small employers. During a crisis, only the extra layoffs above the normal level are blamed on it.

      Layoffs by floor and by kind. JOLTS counts about 13 to 21 layoffs and discharges per 100 workers a year, so they are common, but they are not all the same. Who is hit follows unemployment rates by occupation year by year (BLS Current Population Survey, Table 25, 2002–2025): entry-level jobs about 25 per 100 a year before 2008 and about 18 now, directors and executives about 8 to 10, with managers hit relatively harder in 2008–10. Career stories tell three kinds apart. About 4 in 10 layoffs are temporary, with a recall to the same job weeks later, most often in construction, manufacturing, hotels and restaurants, and transport (relative chances by industry are our assumption). Some short lower-level jobs simply end ("their temporary job ends"): among unemployed job losers, about 19% had finished a temporary job (BLS, September 2026). The rest are permanent layoffs.

      Quitting and job hunting. A worker may quit at any time and walk their floor looking for something better. The chance is highest in the first months at a new employer and falls with tenure, as in US worker data (Farber); layoffs also hit newcomers hardest, and they hit lower floors more often than higher ones: entry level about 2.5 times as often as directors and executives (BLS Current Population Survey, 2025). About 4 in 10 layoffs end with a recall to the same job at the same employer, as in US data (Fujita and Moscarini), and a recalled worker keeps their tenure. The chance follows Washington state's published resignation rate for its own staff; every other company uses its industry's real quit and layoff rates for that month from the BLS JOLTS survey (averaged over 2001–2026: quits from about 0.7% a month in government to 3.5% in leisure and hospitality, layoffs from about 0.5% in government to 3.1% in construction), adjusted slightly for company size. Job searches are shorter where the industry has more openings. Among the visible figures, job hoppers quit more often and nepo hires rarely; the statistical sample has no personas. People who quit never have to take a lower job: if no seat on their floor is free, they keep looking until one is. About 1 in 20 quitters above entry level choose a lower job anyway (our estimate, informed by Pew Research's 2022 survey of people who quit in 2021). Most people who quit already have their next job lined up: in September 2026 only 741,000 of the unemployed had quit their last job, against 3.1 million quits a month (BLS). So about 9 in 10 simulated quitters move on after a handover of two to six weeks (our estimate from those figures). Everyone else, including everyone laid off, searches for as long as real unemployment lasts. The BLS Current Population Survey (September 2026) reports a median of 11.5 weeks for people unemployed now, but that snapshot over-represents long searches, so the model uses the completed lengths consistent with it: half end within about 5 weeks, most within a few months, and a few take a year or more. During the 2008 crisis and COVID they last about twice as long, as they did in reality. Most moves between companies are sideways: about 1 in 5 promotions come from changing employer, as in real data (in Finnish employer records, promotions from another firm are 4.3% of job entries against 24.5% for internal promotions, DeVaro et al.; among US MBA graduates, 77% of moves into bigger roles were internal, Bidwell and Mollick 2015). About 85% of those step-ups stay in the worker's own industry, because people moving between firms usually keep the same job title (DeVaro et al.); the model has no occupations, so the industry stands in for the field.

      Missing: the measures that would show stagnation directly, such as time spent at one level before promotion, the share of senior roles filled internally, and mid-level turnover. Most companies don't publish them.

      Settings: what comes from data and what is assumed

      Every rule that shapes a career is listed here. Data: the value is taken from a published source. Fitted: the form is ours, but its values were set so the simulated workforce reproduces a published figure, and that figure is checked on every test run. Assumption: no public data pins it down, so the value is our judgement. No calibration factors: until version 30, quits were scaled up 11% and layoffs 7% to reach the JOLTS level; the causes were found and fixed (see the release notes), and the rates now come out within about 2% of JOLTS on their own. Removed in version 31 because no data supported them: a lower layoff rate for firms in their first two years (young firms actually cut jobs more often, Census Business Dynamics Statistics) and extra layoffs at firms in transition.

      Version 44 · Clearer floors, steadier climbs, and bugs from the reviews 21 Oct 2026 · current

      • "Senior" is now "Professional". The floor stands for professional and business-specialist jobs (teacher, nurse, accountant), which graduates hold from their first job. All four reviewers read "Senior" as seniority and marked down first jobs there. Nothing in the simulation changed.
      • Promotions at the top take longer: at least 2 years from Lead to Director and 3 from Director to Executive (one year below Lead, where young first-line managers are common). Reviewers flagged a climb from Junior to Director in three years. LinkedIn histories average 30 months between promotions.
      • Each break has a reason, drawn by age from what people out of the workforce say (CPS): mostly study under 25, family or health from 25.
      • A job lined up always starts. If every seat filled during the notice period, the offer still stands; a career no longer shows "job lined up" followed by "keeps looking". If the run ends during the notice period, the journey says the new job is about to start.

      Version 43 · Fixes from four independent reviews of 40 careers 21 Oct 2026

      • Reviewed by four AI models. Gemini, ChatGPT, Grok and Claude each scored 40 followed careers. Their scores agreed broadly (correlations 0.74–0.79). The fixes below are the points they shared that data backs; the points where the data disagrees with them are listed in the release notes too.
      • No management first jobs, and first jobs by age. Under 22, first jobs follow what 16–19-year-olds do (10% professional); from 22, what 20–24-year-olds do, without management, since those managers mostly have years of work behind them (CPS). Promotions under 30 were refitted to the share of young managers.
      • Breaks are spread more evenly. One person's breaks are capped at 60% of their job endings, so the time out of work, which still matches the surveys, is not piled on a few people.
      • Temporary layoffs follow industry data. Recall rates by industry from BLS Mass Layoff Statistics: high in leisure, health care and construction, low in information and very low in finance.
      • Fewer step-downs after a layoff: 25%, from the Displaced Workers Survey (about 1 in 4 earned 20% or more less in their new job), instead of our 40%.
      • Public employers have a place: moving between state or local governments in different states is now rare.
      • The Regular employee is the norm (the middle of the workforce); the Settler now settles much more (the bottom 15%).
      • Speed has no effect on careers. At high speed a few days between checks were not counted toward promotions; they are now. The effect was under 2%.
      • Months in the journey. When notice and the new job fall either side of New Year, both show the month.
      • Where the data disagreed with the reviews: changing industry is normal (about 60–65% of job changes, Census); staying at entry or junior level into your 40s is common (about half of 45–54-year-olds, CPS); and these regular employees change employer less often than the average American at every age (NLSY79).

      Version 39 · How people really change jobs 21 Oct 2026

      • Three ways a job ends. Straight to another job after a short notice period, into unemployment and a search, or a break out of the workforce (study, family, health), in the proportions of the monthly US labour-force flows: about 40% direct moves, 20% unemployment and 40% breaks (Fujita, Moscarini and Postel-Vinay; CPS flows via FRED). Breaks last about a year and are mostly taken before 40; people aged 18–38 now spend about 18% of their weeks out of the workforce, as in the NLSY97 survey.       • No more jumping figures. A figure that falls behind walks faster to catch up, instead of jumping to its next place.
      • Still off. Older workers move a little too often (tenure at 45–54 about 4.5 years against 7), and the frequent movers settle down with age less than real ones do. The comparator lists both.

      Version 27 · Real turnover, year by year 19 Oct 2026

      • Quits and layoffs follow the real monthly rates. Instead of a fixed level with a multiplier during each crisis, the whole economy now rises and falls with the BLS JOLTS rates for every month from 2001 to August 2026: the dot-com bust, the 2008 crisis, the low-churn 2010s, the Great Resignation and the cooling since.       • Ages and retirement. Workers have ages, retire at about 62 and change jobs more often when young.
      • Tenure and floors matter. New hires are the likeliest to quit or be laid off; lower floors are laid off more often; 4 in 10 layoffs end in a recall to the same job.

      Version 26 · A sample that matches the US economy 19 Oct 2026

      • Company sizes match reality. 93% of simulated workers used to work at companies with 100,000 or more staff, against about 9% in reality, and almost none at small firms. 34 real employers were added, with headcounts from annual reports or official figures, so every industry has a company in every size group, and each company now stands for its industry and size group. Firms under 100 staff now employ about a third of private-sector workers, as in Census and BLS data, and no single company holds more than its real share.
      • Every company has every career stage. With realistic company sizes, the old rule (small companies stop at Junior) left only 0.2% of workers able to reach Executive, against 2.4% in reality, because small firms have owners and managers too. Ladders now all reach Executive; what differs is how many seats each floor has, set by industry (BLS occupation data), size and career score. Rung spacing shows how hard each step is, and width shows company size. Floor labels are the plain role names.
      • Promotions mostly happen inside the company. 71% of promotions used to come from switching employer, and 70% of those crossed industries (a Junior at Starbucks becoming a Senior at Cisco).       Version 25 · Quitters don't step down 19 Oct 2026

      • People who quit rarely take a lower job. A worker who quit and found no free seat on their floor used to take a lower job after a second search. Now they keep looking in short rounds until a seat on their own floor opens. About 1 in 20 quitters above entry level still choose a lower job. The 1 in 20 is our estimate: Pew Research found that 9% of college graduates and 18% of other workers who quit in 2021 had fewer chances to advance in their new job, and moving down a floor is a bigger step than that. People who are laid off still step down when they need to.

      Version 21 · Real job-search lengths 18 Oct 2026

      Versions 1–5 · The model 18 Oct 2026

      • Stacked floors and ladders; one ladder per real company, all starting at the ground; heights from sourced headcounts and colours from Glassdoor career scores; FDJ United and Dussmann screened and added.

      Known limitations

      • No official data tracks promotions economy-wide; the model's level-to-level promotions (about 11–16 per 100 workers a year under 35, 5–7 at 35–56) sit below the broader self-reported promotion rates in the NLSY (about 16–18% a year at 23–33), which also count promotions within the same level.
      • Headcount histories cover the largest companies; smaller companies founded before 2000 appear at today's size. Some early values and some US workforce shares are estimates.
      • Turnover by company size uses one gradient for all years (BLS JOLTS by establishment size, 2025–26). How the gap between small and large firms changed from year to year (Census Business Dynamics Statistics) could not be downloaded here.
      • Young workers still change employer a little too often: about 1.06 new jobs a year at 18–23 against 0.85 in the NLSY97 survey, and 0.41 at 30–38 against 0.32. Workers aged 55–64 have a median of about 7–8 years with their employer, against 9.6 in the BLS tenure survey. Total quits are fixed by JOLTS, so lowering one age group's moves raises another's; the current setting is the best balance found.
      • About 18% of workers lose a job without a recall over three normal years (2005–07), against 8.5% in the Displaced Workers Survey. The survey is known to undercount job loss, because people forget job losses (recall bias; Flaaen, Shapiro and Sorkin), and it leaves out dismissals, which JOLTS layoffs include. So the comparator scores how job loss rises in downturns instead: in the survey it was 1.9 times its normal level in 2007–09; in the model about 1.2–1.3 times.
      • About 9% of simulated careers (8–10% per run, down from about 11%) contain a stretch that looks unusual: four or more new employers in one year after 25, three permanent job losses within three years after 30, three or more step-downs, or three or more years out of work. Such careers exist, but with no survey benchmark for them, the model probably has a few too many, mostly among frequent movers.
      • Without calibration, quits come out about 2% above JOLTS and layoffs about 1% above, averaged over 2001–2025. That residual is not corrected; it is far smaller than the year-to-year noise of a single run.
      • Several small-firm groups (leisure, health, professional services, construction and finance under 100 staff) rest on one or two listed companies, because very few firms that small publish a headcount. They carry their group's real rates, but their own histories say little about it.
      • Promotions inside a company do not get faster on green ladders. In the model they come from free seats, and seats open when people above leave, so high-turnover groups (often red) promote slightly more often (about 6.4 against 5.1 per 100 a year). Green ladders mean fewer layoffs and easier moves up by changing employer. No public data measures internal promotion rates by industry and year, so this link is not tuned to data.
      • The groups mirror their industries only approximately: over 2000–2025 their combined headcount typically stays within 2–11% of the industry's real path. Finance, information, professional services and construction have no company with 100,000 or more US staff whose history follows the industry, so those jobs go to the next size group.
      • Short events like the two-month COVID shock give noisier percentages than long ones, even with the large sample, and so do small industries in a single year.
      • JOLTS publishes turnover by establishment size, not firm size; the translation to firm size is our crosswalk. Company stability (credit ratings) is planned for a later version.
      • How much harder a step is in small or large firms (the size factors for seats) is our assumption; only the direction comes from research. The split of firms with 10,000 or more staff into 10,000–99,999 and 100,000 or more is our estimate.
      • Small companies each stand for a very large group of jobs, so a 45-person firm can appear in many careers.
      • Government is matched by level of government, not by employer size, because no national data on public employer sizes exists. The City of Phoenix headcount dates from 2019.
