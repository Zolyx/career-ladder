Six Floors Proof of concept

Proof of concept. The companies, events and dates are real and sourced. The stick figures are fictional workers whose careers are simulated: they illustrate what typically happened to people like them, not what happened to any real person. Don't use it for real career or investment decisions.

Vibe-coded. Designed and directed by Zolyx, and built with Claude (Anthropic) in a single conversation, without writing code by hand. Sources are cited throughout, but the model hasn't been reviewed by a labour economist.

Each ladder is a real company. Every company has all six career stages, from entry level to executive, but not the same number of seats on each: rung spacing shows how hard each step is there (dense rungs, many seats above; sparse rungs, few). Width is company size. Colour is how employees rate their career opportunities on Glassdoor. The companies are a sample chosen to match the real US economy by industry and company size. The stick figures are simulated workers who climb, switch companies, start over, or lose their footing when a company collapses and its ladder vanishes.

        How each crisis shifted this run

        Each event is compared with the years just before it, in this same simulation, next to the real US change for the same comparison. The workers are simulated, so exact numbers change on every restart; the direction of the shift is what to look at. Figures are per year, so periods of different length compare fairly. They fill in as the run reaches each period.

        What the data says

        Big companies are not rated better

        The widest ladders, companies with 100,000 or more staff, score between 3.1 (Target) and 4.2 (Google). Only Google is clearly green. The best scores in the model belong to consulting firms with 20,000 to 40,000 staff.

        Nvidia is the outlier

        Highest career score in the set at 4.4 from over 7,000 reviews, and a reported turnover of 3.7% in fiscal 2026. At about 42,000 staff it is still far smaller than Walmart or Amazon.

        Intern to CEO, one ladder

        Michael Fiddelke joined Target as a finance intern in 2003 and became CEO in February 2026, the red figure in the model. He arrived with an MBA from Kellogg and consulting experience at Deloitte.

        Three ways to the top

        Fiddelke (red) climbed Target's ladder rung by rung for 23 years. Elkann (gold), grandson of Fiat's Gianni Agnelli, was lifted onto Fiat's board at 21. Huang (green) washed dishes at Denny's, then built his own ladder: Nvidia grew from 392 staff in 2000 to about 42,000.

        Size doesn't protect a ladder

        Lehman Brothers had about 26,200 staff and Enron about 20,600 when they failed. Both were about as large as adidas or Nvidia are today.

        What's real and what's simulated

        Real, the same every time

        The companies, their sizes, career scores and founding years. Every event and its date: Lehman's collapse, Chrysler's bankruptcy, the Stellantis merger, 9/11 and the 2008 crisis. The careers of Michael Fiddelke, John Elkann, Jensen Huang and Jimmy Donaldson. The overall shifts the crises caused, such as US quits falling about 40% in 2008.

        Simulated, different on every restart

        The 135 stick figures. No record exists of every employee's career, so the model creates fictional workers and gives them rules tuned to the real figures where data exists. Each simulated year it rolls the dice for each one: promoted or not, quit or not, laid off or not, which company hires them. This is an agent-based simulation.

        What that means

        The model illustrates rather than predicts. It doesn't say "this person at Lehman lost their job". It says "a typical worker there in 2008 would have faced something like this". Pressing Restart replays the same history with a fresh roll of the dice, so individual stories change while the patterns stay. A single run can be skewed by luck; an effect that shows up on every restart is a real effect of the rules.

        Which companies count in the economy graph

        The economy chart and the crisis table only count workers at a reference sample: companies whose course through 9/11, the 2008 crisis and COVID-19 was close to the average experience of the economy. Companies that failed, boomed, or were hit far harder than average are left out, whatever their size or how well documented they are. Every company still appears in the 3D model and the log.

        How to read it, and what it can't tell you

        Every company has every career stage. Every ladder starts on the ground floor, L0 Entry level, and reaches L5 Executive, because every company, however small, has someone in charge: in a 40-person firm that is the owner or general manager, who the BLS counts as a top executive. What differs is how many seats each floor has, so the same step can be easy at one company and very hard at another. Rung spacing shows this: dense rungs below a floor mean many seats on it, sparse rungs mean few. A ladder's width shows the company's size. Until version 25, height showed company size and small companies stopped at Junior; once the sample matched real company sizes, that rule left only 0.2% of workers able to reach Executive, against 2.4% in reality.

        Every floor has a limited number of seats. A company can only have so many directors and executives, so nobody, nepo hires included, can move onto a floor unless a seat there is free. Across the whole economy, seats follow the proportions of real US jobs from the BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (May 2025): top executives and general managers about 2.4% of all jobs, other managers 4.8%, supervisors and team leads about 5% (our estimate), professional roles 25.8%, and the rest split evenly between junior and entry-level work. Each company's own mix differs in three ways. Industry: Senior seats follow the industry's share of professional and business specialist jobs (BLS Current Population Survey, September 2026), and the management floors its share of managers (BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, May 2023): 58% of jobs in education and health are professional, against 4% in construction, so becoming Senior is far easier in a hospital than on a building site; managers are 13.6% of jobs in finance but 3.9% in transportation. Size: bigger firms have more layers of management (French employer data, Caliendo, Monte and Rossi-Hansberg 2015), so a small firm has an owner but almost no directors, and a large one many directors; the exact factors are our assumption. Career score: the better the score, the larger the share of seats from Senior upwards (our assumption, since no public data gives each company's mix of senior roles). All companies are then calibrated together so the economy as a whole keeps the BLS proportions. When a seat opens, someone from the floor below usually fills it within about a year, as one departure sets off a chain of promotions; climbers push about 1.5 times as hard for it, and nepo hires 2.6 times as hard where their family controls the company (Walmart, by the Walton family; Volkswagen and Porsche, by the Porsche and Piëch families through Porsche SE; Fiat Chrysler and Stellantis, by the Agnelli family through Exor; Dussmann; and, before they collapsed, Schlecker and Arcadia Group) but only 1.4 times elsewhere. Overall, the simulated workforce stays close to the real pyramid: about 33% entry level, 30% junior, 25% senior, 5% leads, 4% directors and 2% executives, against 31, 31, 26, 5, 4.8 and 2.4%.

        Staying in the same field. About 37 to 39% of US job-to-job moves stay in the same industry sector (Census Bureau Job-to-Job Flows, as analysed by the Richmond Fed for 2019 to 2022), and the model keeps a similar share. Every industry is now represented by several companies of different sizes, so a worker can stay in their field without returning to the employer they left. Staying in the field also follows the same rebalancing as hiring, so each industry keeps close to its real share of jobs. Workers passed over for promotion usually look for a better-rated company in their own industry. Career changers always start over in a different industry from the one they left.

        Colour is perception. Glassdoor reviewers choose to post, so scores lean towards people with strong feelings. Scores from a few dozen reviews can swing widely. Large companies mix very different jobs into one number.

        The simulation is a set of rules, not data. Each simulated year, a worker may be promoted if a seat on the floor above is free at their company; the chance is scaled by the company's career score and cut if the company is in transition. Ladders appear in the year the company was founded and vanish in the year it collapsed. When a ladder vanishes, about half of its workers find a job on the same floor elsewhere; the rest drop a floor or back to entry level. Where these numbers are not calibrated to data, they are illustrative assumptions chosen to make the dynamics visible.

        Mergers. PSA Group (Peugeot, Citroën, Opel) and Fiat Chrysler merged into Stellantis on 16 January 2021. In the simulation their two ladders slide together and become one double ladder, two ladders glued along a shared middle rail. Workers move over with them: about 65% keep their floor, about 25% are moved down a floor because the merged company has duplicate roles, and about 10% are laid off to the ground. Those shares are illustrative assumptions. The predecessors take Stellantis's colour, since they no longer have reviews of their own.

        The transition rule. A company is marked "in transition" when it has announced major job cuts, a restructuring, a large merger still being integrated, or a review that could lead to selling or closing parts of the business, within the last 12 months. In the simulation, the ladder only fades and slows promotions from the month the transition began: February 2025 for Porsche, when its first job cuts were announced, and July 2026 for FDJ United, when it began reviewing its online business. Before that they behave like any other company.

        Growing ladders. Ladders widen as companies grow. Nvidia's follows its real headcount history: 392 staff in January 2000, past 1,000 in 2002 and past 10,000 in 2017. Companies founded after 2000, such as Airbnb, Spotify, Zalando, Duolingo, United Homes Group and MrBeast, start narrow in their founding year and widen to today's size, with estimated timing that assumes fast early growth that slows later. The biggest older companies now follow their real headcount histories too, from annual reports: Amazon grows from about 9,000 staff in 2000 to 1.5 million, Google from under 300 in 2001 to about 190,000, Accenture from about 71,000 to 779,000, Infosys from about 5,000 to 328,000; the US Postal Service shrinks from about 925,000 to 624,000. Smaller older companies are shown at today's size. A company's size group can change as it grows or shrinks, and with it the group of jobs it stands for.

        Company histories and US workforces. Documented job cuts at sample companies happen on their real dates, on top of their industry's normal rate: Cisco's 8,500 in 2001, United Airlines' 20,000 after 9/11, Starbucks' 12,000 in 2008, Chrysler's bankruptcy in 2009, JPMorgan's 9,200 after taking over Washington Mutual, Volkswagen's 30,000 after the diesel scandal, Google's 12,000 and Accenture's 19,000 in 2023, and others. Each hits the same share of the company's simulated workers as of its real staff at the time. Because the model's economy is the United States, each company hires in proportion to its US workforce and is placed in the size group of that workforce, as the Census counts firm size: Siemens has more than 45,000 US staff, Stellantis about 50,000. Companies with no US operations, such as Schlecker, Air Berlin or Zalando, stay in the model as ladders and events but hire no simulated workers. Some US shares are our estimates.

        The events are markers only. 9/11, the 2008 financial crisis, COVID-19 and the Great Resignation are shown as shaded bands and banners, but they change nothing in the model: no rate, ratio or share depends on them, and switching them off changes only what is displayed. Everything that moves over time comes from real dated data: quits and layoffs from JOLTS month by month, job-search length from the yearly median duration of unemployment, the industry mix by year, layoffs by floor from occupation data by year, and documented company events on their dates. So the events show what the numbers did at those times: after 9/11, quits fell about 12% while layoffs barely moved; in 2008–10, quits fell about a third, layoffs rose about a fifth and job searches more than doubled; in March–April 2020 layoffs jumped about sixfold, unevenly across industries (in the worst month restaurants, hotels and entertainment laid off about 28% of their staff, government about 1%; JOLTS, BLS Monthly Labor Review, June 2021). New events can be added as markers without touching any number. Layoffs above the normal level hit workers broadly rather than mainly the frequent movers, as recession layoffs reach settled workers too, and every quit and layoff is drawn ahead using each month's real rate and placed on the date it happens. Most companies survived all three crises, but some crashed: Sabena after 9/11; Lehman Brothers, Washington Mutual and Circuit City in 2008; Flybe and Arcadia Group during COVID-19, alongside Chrysler's bankruptcy and the airline cuts after 9/11. Their ladders break apart in the model, but everything that happens at failed companies (promotions, quits and the mass layoffs when they collapse) is kept out of the economy chart and the impact table, because extreme cases would distort the picture of the typical worker. Promotions have no event effect: they slow down in bad times only because fewer people quit and fewer seats open.

        Turnover over the years. Quits and layoffs follow the BLS JOLTS survey month by month from 2001 to August 2026: each industry keeps its own level, and the whole economy rises and falls with the real monthly rates. Quits ran at about 26 per 100 workers a year in 2001, fell to 16 in 2009, climbed to 28 in 2019 and peaked at about 33 in 2021–22, then eased to about 24. Layoffs ran at about 18 a year in the early 2000s, 21 in 2009, and fell to 12–13 after the pandemic. A move straight to another employer counts as a quit, as it does in JOLTS. Across 26 years the simulated yearly rates come within a few percent of the real ones on average and follow the same ups and downs (correlation about 0.95). The mix of jobs changes over time too: manufacturing held about 13% of US jobs in 2000 and about 8% today, while education and health grew from about 12% to 18% (BLS Current Employment Statistics and Employment Projections; 2005 and 2020 interpolated).

        Industry mix and hiring. Simulated workers are hired so that each industry gets its real share of US jobs in that year (Bureau of Labor Statistics); today that is education and health 18%, government 15%, leisure and hospitality 14%, professional and business services 14%, trade 14%, manufacturing 8%, finance 6%, construction 6%, transportation 5%, and information, where most tech companies sit, under 2%. Inside an industry, jobs are also split by company size in real proportions (see below), each company sharing its size group's jobs with the others in it in proportion to its size, and a better career score makes a company somewhat more attractive. So a tech company can appear in the model as a ladder without taking a bigger share of the workforce than tech really has. Companies' sizes change over time where their growth is modelled.

        Is the sample representative? A handful of companies can't contain the US economy, so each company stands for a group of jobs: its industry and its size group. Real US jobs are split over those groups and each group's jobs are shared by the companies in it. A 38-person manufacturer like Electro-Sensors therefore stands for all manufacturing jobs in firms under 100 staff (about 2.4% of US jobs), and Walmart can take no more than its share of retail jobs in the largest firms. Every industry has a company in every size group. Industry shares come from the BLS (September 2026). Company sizes come from Census and BLS firm data: firms under 100 staff hold about 34% of private-sector jobs (BLS Business Employment Dynamics, Q1 2025: 34.1%), firms under 500 about 46% (Census Statistics of U.S. Businesses, 2021: 45.9%), firms with 10,000 or more about 30% (Census, 2019). The split between 10,000–99,999 and 100,000 or more (21% and 9%) and the share of firms with 500–999 staff are our estimates. Each industry's share in small firms also comes from Census data: 82% of construction jobs are in firms under 500 staff, against 28% in finance. Government is matched by level instead (federal 11%, state 23%, local 65%; BLS, September 2026), because there is no national data on the size of public employers. Headcounts of the companies added for this come from their annual reports (10-K) or official figures. In a full run the simulated workforce stays within about 2 points of every industry's real share and within about 2 points of every size group's.

        Reference employers. To keep the sample representative, the model includes large, typical employers from sectors that were missing: Walmart (retail), HCA Healthcare (health care), JPMorgan Chase (finance), Marriott and Starbucks (hospitality and food service), the US Postal Service (government) and VINCI (construction). Companies best known for one extreme event, such as General Motors' bankruptcy, Citigroup's 52,000 job cuts in 2008 or Boeing's 9/11 layoffs, were left out on purpose. Version 26 added 34 employers to fill every industry and size group, from Progressive and Cheesecake Factory to Fairfax County Public Schools and small listed firms such as AMREP and Hennessy Advisors.

        Age, retirement and a settled start. Every simulated worker has an age. Newcomers start between 18 and 26, and people retire at about 62 on average, rarely before 55 or after 70 (Gallup's average retirement age is 61–62). Young workers change jobs far more often than older ones, following the US National Longitudinal Survey of Youth (NLSY79): about 0.8 new jobs a year at 18–24, falling to about 0.2 at 45 and over. Retirees are replaced by newcomers at entry level, as in a real workforce. Before the run starts in 2000, the statistical sample quietly lives through 20 years of job changes so it begins in a realistic, settled state; then every worker is given an age from the real age mix of employed Americans in 2000 (median about 39), older workers more often on higher floors. As the baby boomers age, the median rises to about 42 by the mid-2020s, as it did in reality. Promotions also come mostly early in a career (Pergamit and Veum; McCue), so the median age on each floor ends up close to BLS occupation data: about 33 at entry level, 46 for managers and 52 at the top (chief executives: 51.6).

        Movers and stayers. A small group of workers changes jobs again and again, while most stay put for years. Each simulated worker has a lasting tendency to move, drawn at random; layoffs are even more concentrated on the frequent movers than quits, because most layoffs and discharges counted by JOLTS are recalls, dismissals and the end of short, seasonal or temporary jobs. Only a few per 100 workers a year lose a job they had settled into: the Displaced Workers Survey finds 7–10% of workers over three normal years and about 16% in 2007–09 (Farber). The gap between movers and stayers narrows with age. The result matches the BLS tenure survey (January 2024): a median of about 4 years with the current employer and about a quarter of workers with 10 years or more. These factors only decide who is let go within a floor and an industry; how often each floor and each industry is hit comes from the data, so no industry's layoff rate drifts from its BLS level. In career stories, a company with fewer than 100 staff is named as "a small firm like …", because it stands for many small employers. During a crisis, only the extra layoffs above the normal level are blamed on it.

        Layoffs by floor and by kind. JOLTS counts about 13 to 21 layoffs and discharges per 100 workers a year, so they are common, but they are not all the same. Who is hit follows unemployment rates by occupation year by year (BLS Current Population Survey, Table 25, 2002–2025): entry-level jobs about 25 per 100 a year before 2008 and about 18 now, directors and executives about 8 to 10, with managers hit relatively harder in 2008–10. Career stories tell three kinds apart. About 4 in 10 layoffs are temporary, with a recall to the same job weeks later, most often in construction, manufacturing, hotels and restaurants, and transport (relative chances by industry are our assumption). Some short lower-level jobs simply end ("their temporary job ends"): among unemployed job losers, about 19% had finished a temporary job (BLS, September 2026). The rest are permanent layoffs. Firms in their first two years rarely cut jobs (our assumption).

        Quitting and job hunting. A worker may quit at any time and walk their floor looking for something better. The chance is highest in the first months at a new employer and falls with tenure, as in US worker data (Farber); layoffs also hit newcomers hardest, and they hit lower floors more often than higher ones: entry level about 2.5 times as often as directors and executives (BLS Current Population Survey, 2025). About 4 in 10 layoffs end with a recall to the same job at the same employer, as in US data (Fujita and Moscarini), and a recalled worker keeps their tenure. The chance follows the company's published attrition rate where there is one: about 13 to 14% at TCS, Infosys, Wipro and Accenture, and 3.7% at Nvidia. Every other company uses its industry's real quit rate from the BLS JOLTS survey, from 0.8% a month in government to 3.4% a month in leisure and hospitality. Layoffs in normal years follow each industry's real layoff rate the same way, from 0.4% a month in government to 2.0% in professional and business services. Job hoppers quit more often, nepo hires rarely. People who quit never have to take a lower job: if no seat on their floor is free, they keep looking until one is. About 1 in 20 quitters above entry level choose a lower job anyway (our estimate, informed by Pew Research's 2022 survey of people who quit in 2021). Most people who quit already have their next job lined up: in September 2026 only 741,000 of the unemployed had quit their last job, against 3.1 million quits a month (BLS). So about 9 in 10 simulated quitters move on after a handover of two to six weeks (our estimate from those figures). Everyone else, including everyone laid off, searches for as long as real unemployment lasts. The BLS Current Population Survey (September 2026) reports a median of 11.5 weeks for people unemployed now, but that snapshot over-represents long searches, so the model uses the completed lengths consistent with it: half end within about 5 weeks, most within a few months, and a few take a year or more. During the 2008 crisis and COVID they last about twice as long, as they did in reality. Most moves between companies are sideways: about 1 in 5 promotions come from changing employer, as in real data (in Finnish employer records, promotions from another firm are 4.3% of job entries against 24.5% for internal promotions, DeVaro et al.; among US MBA graduates, 77% of moves into bigger roles were internal, Bidwell and Mollick 2015). About 85% of those step-ups stay in the worker's own industry, because people moving between firms usually keep the same job title (DeVaro et al.); the model has no occupations, so the industry stands in for the field.

        Consulting layoff rates are approximate. Bain, BCG, McKinsey and the IT services firms use the layoff rate of the whole professional and business services industry, which includes temporary staffing agencies with very high turnover. BLS publishes no separate rate for consulting, so the model doesn't invent one; their real layoff rates are probably lower.

        Why consulting scores so high. Bain, BCG and McKinsey run an "up or out" model: consultants are promoted on a fixed schedule or expected to leave. That makes career progression fast and visible for those who stay, which shows in their scores, but it also means many people exit after a few years.

        Why the narrowest ladders are grey. Companies with a few hundred staff or fewer rarely have enough employee reviews for a reliable score: Telegram, Craigslist, Electro-Sensors, AMREP and the other small firms in the sample have no usable Glassdoor career rating, so their ladders stay grey rather than being given a guessed colour. In the simulation they count as an average-rated employer.

        Missing: the measures that would show stagnation directly, such as time spent at one level before promotion, the share of senior roles filled internally, and mid-level turnover. Most companies don't publish them.

        Version 25 · Quitters don't step down 19 Oct 2026

        • People who quit rarely take a lower job. A worker who quit and found no free seat on their floor used to take a lower job after a second search. Now they keep looking in short rounds until a seat on their own floor opens. About 1 in 20 quitters above entry level still choose a lower job. The 1 in 20 is our estimate: Pew Research found that 9% of college graduates and 18% of other workers who quit in 2021 had fewer chances to advance in their new job, and moving down a floor is a bigger step than that. People who are laid off still step down when they need to.

        Version 24 · Typical careers first 19 Oct 2026

        • A new "Typical worker" scenario. The worker you follow is picked at random from the 2000 workforce, the same way as everyone else in the model: 9 in 10 already have a job, at a company and on a floor drawn in real proportions; the rest are looking for a first job. Nothing is scripted.
        • "Surprise me" is mostly typical. It used to pick each of the five scenarios equally often, so 2 in 5 runs were a collapse or a merger. Now it follows a typical worker 80% of the time, a first job 10%, Infosys or Bain 4% each, and the Lehman collapse or the Fiat Chrysler merger only 1% each. These odds are our choice; collapses and mergers can still happen naturally to anyone.

        Version 23 · Same field, real ratings, career score that matters 18 Oct 2026

        • Most careers stay closer to one field. About 9 in 10 job moves used to change industry, about what random picks give. Now about 40% stay in the same industry, close to the 37 to 39% in Census job-to-job data.
        • The career score changes how far you can get. Better-rated companies have a larger share of seats from Senior upwards, so their workers end up higher on average. The economy-wide pyramid stays the same.
        • Two fixes. "Moves to X, which is rated better" now only appears when X really has a higher career score. Career changers now always start over in a different industry; one could restart at the company they had just left.

        Version 22 · Scenarios always reach their event 18 Oct 2026

        • The followed worker lives through the collapse or merger. In "Their company collapses" and "Their company merges", the worker you follow can no longer be laid off before the event, so they are always at Lehman Brothers in September 2008 or at Fiat Chrysler in January 2021. From the moment the event hits them, everything works as normal, and the outcome is where they are in 2026. The other scenarios (ordinary career, high-attrition employer, best-rated employer) have no single event to wait for, so nothing changes there.
        • Followed workers can be hit by the COVID layoff wave. They used to be exempt from it; now they face the same chance as everyone else.

        Version 21 · Real job-search lengths 18 Oct 2026

        • Searches last as long as real ones. Job hunts used to last one to two years, an illustrative guess that left far too many simulated workers out of work. They now follow BLS data on how long unemployment lasts: half end within about 11.5 weeks, the average is about 25 weeks. About 9 in 10 people who quit have the next job lined up and move on within weeks, as BLS figures suggest. Newcomers replacing retirees take a real-length search for their first job. Together this cuts the share of simulated workers out of work from about 40% to about 12%. Quit and layoff rates, seats and the reference sample are unchanged.

        Version 20 · Seats on every floor 18 Oct 2026

        • Nobody gets a seat that doesn't exist. Each floor of each company now has a limited number of seats, in the proportions of real US jobs (BLS, May 2025), so only about 2% of workers can be Executive at once. Promotions, job switches and hires all need a free seat; job seekers who can't find one on their floor eventually step down.
        • Family connections need a family company. Nepo hires start as Senior and climb fast only where their family controls the company, such as Walmart, Volkswagen, Porsche or Stellantis. Elsewhere they start as Junior with a small edge. Climbers now compete harder for open seats than regular employees.
        • The workforce starts as a pyramid. Workers already in place in 2000 are spread over the floors in the same proportions, instead of crowding the upper floors.

        Version 19 · Floors as career potential 18 Oct 2026

        • Floor names describe what you can reach. The floors now read "You can become Junior" up to "You can become Executive", with "Entry level: everyone starts here" on the ground. A ladder's height shows how far you can progress at that company. It's still estimated from company size; basing it on each company's real role structure is the planned next step.

        Version 18 · Surprise scenarios 18 Oct 2026

        • Reveal names the worker type. The panel says which type of worker it was, describes their real start (including a nepo hire's family connections and where the first job was) and how that type behaves, and phrases the ending clearly.
        • Follow one career can keep the scenario secret. "Surprise me" picks one of the five scenarios at random. During the run the journey log stays hidden; when the simulation reaches 2026, a panel reveals which scenario it was and how the career ended, and the full journey unlocks.

        Version 17 · Real turnover by industry 18 Oct 2026

        • Quits and layoffs follow each industry's real rates. The assumed 10% yearly quit chance and 4% layoff chance are replaced by the monthly rates the BLS JOLTS survey measures for each industry. A hotel or restaurant worker now changes jobs about four times as often as a government worker, as in reality. Companies with their own published attrition keep it.
        • Hiring replaces turnover. Industries that lose people faster also hire more, so each keeps its real share of jobs.
        • Industry balance includes retirement. Hiring now replaces everyone who leaves an industry, retirees included, which brought government back to its real share.
        • Balanced starting workforce. In 2000 workers are placed by each industry's real share of jobs, and hiring gently favours any industry that drifts below its real share, as real labour markets rebalance. This fixed government, which had been running about 4 points short.
        • Retirement. About 2.5% of workers retire each year (a roughly 40-year career) and a newcomer starts at entry level, so the workforce doesn't drift upward over 26 years.
        • Warm-up. The statistical sample lives through 20 quiet years before 2000, so the run starts from a settled workforce instead of an artificial starting state.
        • COVID layoff wave. The March–April 2020 layoffs are now applied as one wave at lockdown, sized to the real data, because workers only reconsider their jobs about once a year.
        • Recalibrated: every crisis now matches the direction of the real shift, and 2008 and COVID match its size closely. The crisis shifts still apply on top, and no other features changed.

        Version 16 · A realistic industry mix 18 Oct 2026

        • Hiring follows the real economy. Each industry receives its real share of US jobs, and bigger companies hire proportionally more within their industry. Before, a 30-person company was about as likely to hire a worker as Walmart.
        • Fewer tech ladders. Removed Mozilla, Personio and Celonis. Tech now gets under 2% of the simulated workforce, as in reality, however many tech ladders are shown.
        • Missing industries filled. Added the US Postal Service (government), Starbucks (food service) and VINCI (construction).
        • A checked reference sample. Every company was audited for how closely its course through the three crises followed the average. Companies that failed, boomed (Amazon, Nvidia, MrBeast) or were hit far harder than average (airlines, hotels, Airbnb, the Stellantis group) are left out of the economy chart and crisis table; they stay in the model. Rates are now per 100 workers in that sample. A new section lists which companies count and why the others don't.
        • Crisis casualties. Added companies that crashed in each crisis: Sabena (9/11), Washington Mutual and Circuit City (2008), Flybe and Arcadia Group (COVID-19). Everything at failed companies is now excluded from the economy chart, not just their final layoffs.
        • Collapsed companies listed below the model. Their labels no longer sit on the floors; a dated list under the model fills in as each one fails, and clicking a name shows its details.
        • Industry mix panel. Shows the simulated workforce by industry against the real US share, live.

        Version 15 · A robust, representative sample 18 Oct 2026

        • Three catastrophes, calibrated to the whole economy. 9/11, the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic (plus the Great Resignation that followed) now shift quits and layoffs by the same percentages measured across all US employers in the BLS JOLTS survey. Every company follows the average change.
        • Reference employers instead of extreme cases. Added Walmart, HCA Healthcare, JPMorgan Chase and Marriott to cover retail, health care, finance and hotels. Companies known mainly for one extreme event (General Motors, Citigroup, Boeing) were left out on purpose, so they don't skew the averages.
        • Outliers kept out of the economy chart. Collapses, bankruptcies, merger cuts and airline cuts after 9/11 still happen in the model and the log, but the chart and impact table count only the economy-wide pattern.
        • A large invisible sample, because computers have limits. Drawing more than about 135 animated figures would make the 3D model too slow on ordinary computers, but 135 is too few for reliable statistics. So 2,400 extra workers follow exactly the same rules without being drawn. The charts and the impact table use all of them; the 3D model shows only the 135. Results are given per 100 workers.
        • Real economy chart. Official US hires, quits and layoffs from 2001 to 2026 sit next to the simulated results, and the impact table shows the real change beside each simulated one.
        • Baseline layoffs. Workers now face a small, assumed 4% yearly chance of being laid off in normal years, so crisis changes can be measured against a normal level.

        Version 14 · One timeline 18 Oct 2026

        Versions 1–5 · The model 18 Oct 2026

        • Stacked floors and ladders; one ladder per real company, all starting at the ground; heights from sourced headcounts and colours from Glassdoor career scores; FDJ United and Dussmann screened and added.

        Known limitations

        • No official data tracks promotions economy-wide; the model's level-to-level promotions (about 11–16 per 100 workers a year under 35, 5–7 at 35–56) sit below the broader self-reported promotion rates in the NLSY (about 16–18% a year at 23–33), which also count promotions within the same level.
        • The economy-wide data is for the United States; the model includes European and Asian companies.
        • Headcount histories cover the largest companies; smaller companies founded before 2000 appear at today's size. Some early values and some US workforce shares are estimates.
        • Turnover rises and falls by the same monthly factor in every industry, except in March–April 2020. In reality 2008–10 hit construction and manufacturing harder than the average and health care and government less.
        • Workers over 45 change employer a little more often than in the NLSY79 survey (about 0.24 new jobs a year against 0.19), and workers aged 55–64 have a median of about 7.6 years with their employer against 9.6 in the BLS tenure survey, because layoffs on the upper floors follow occupation unemployment rates, which may overstate managers' layoff risk.
        • About 17% of workers lose a job without a recall over three normal years, against 7–10% in the Displaced Workers Survey. The survey leaves out dismissals and most short temporary jobs, which the model's layoffs include, so part of the gap is definitional.
        • About 1 in 10 simulated careers contains a stretch that looks unusual: four or more new employers in one year after 25, three permanent job losses within three years after 30, three or more step-downs, or three or more years out of work. Such careers exist, but with no survey benchmark for them, the model probably has a few too many, mostly among frequent movers.
        • Short events like the two-month COVID shock give noisier percentages than long ones, even with the large sample, and so do small industries in a single year.
        • Company stability (credit ratings) is planned for a later version.
        • How much harder a step is in small or large firms (the size factors for seats) is our assumption; only the direction comes from research. The split of firms with 10,000 or more staff into 10,000–99,999 and 100,000 or more is our estimate.
        • Small companies each stand for a very large group of jobs, so a 45-person firm can appear in many careers.
        • Government is matched by level of government, not by employer size, because no national data on public employer sizes exists. The City of Phoenix headcount dates from 2019.
