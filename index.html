Six Floors Proof of concept

Proof of concept. The companies, events and dates are real and sourced. The stick figures are fictional workers whose careers are simulated: they illustrate what typically happened to people like them, not what happened to any real person. Don't use it for real career or investment decisions.

Vibe-coded. Designed and directed by Zolyx, and built with Claude (Anthropic) in a single conversation, without writing code by hand. Sources are cited throughout, but the model hasn't been reviewed by a labour economist.

Each ladder is a real company, starting at the ground because every company hires at entry level. Height is how far you can progress there: a ladder that reaches a floor means that at this company, you can become what that floor says. Colour is how employees rate their career opportunities on Glassdoor. The stick figures are simulated workers who climb, switch companies on their own floor, start over, or lose their footing when a company collapses and its ladder vanishes.

        Each event is compared with the years just before it, in this same simulation, next to the real US change for the same comparison. The workers are simulated, so exact numbers change on every restart; the direction of the shift is what to look at. Figures are per year, so periods of different length compare fairly.         What the data says

        Tall ladders are not rated better

        The companies that reach the top floor score between 3.1 (Target) and 4.2 (Google). Only Google is clearly green. The best scores in the model belong to consulting firms one floor lower.

        Nvidia is the outlier

        Highest career score in the set at 4.4 from over 7,000 reviews, and a reported turnover of 3.7% in fiscal 2026. At about 42,000 staff it reaches Director, not the top floor.

        Intern to CEO, one ladder

        Michael Fiddelke joined Target as a finance intern in 2003 and became CEO in February 2026, the red figure in the model. He arrived with an MBA from Kellogg and consulting experience at Deloitte.

        Three ways to the top

        Fiddelke (red) climbed Target's ladder rung by rung for 23 years. Elkann (gold), grandson of Fiat's Gianni Agnelli, was lifted onto Fiat's board at 21. Huang (green) washed dishes at Denny's, then built his own ladder: Nvidia grew from 392 staff in 2000 to about 42,000.

        Size doesn't protect a ladder

        Lehman Brothers had about 26,200 staff and Enron about 20,600 when they failed. Both were as tall as adidas or Nvidia are today.

        What's real and what's simulated

        Real, the same every time

        The companies, their sizes, career scores and founding years. Every event and its date: Lehman's collapse, Chrysler's bankruptcy, the Stellantis merger, 9/11 and the 2008 crisis. The careers of Michael Fiddelke, John Elkann, Jensen Huang and Jimmy Donaldson. The overall shifts the crises caused, such as US quits falling about 40% in 2008.

        Simulated, different on every restart

        The 135 stick figures. No record exists of every employee's career, so the model creates fictional workers and gives them rules tuned to the real figures where data exists. Each simulated year it rolls the dice for each one: promoted or not, quit or not, laid off or not, which company hires them. This is an agent-based simulation.

        What that means

        The model illustrates rather than predicts. It doesn't say "this person at Lehman lost their job". It says "a typical worker there in 2008 would have faced something like this". Pressing Restart replays the same history with a fresh roll of the dice, so individual stories change while the patterns stay. A single run can be skewed by luck; an effect that shows up on every restart is a real effect of the rules.

        Which companies count in the economy graph

        The economy chart and the crisis table only count workers at a reference sample: companies whose course through 9/11, the 2008 crisis and COVID-19 was close to the average experience of the economy. Companies that failed, boomed, or were hit far harder than average are left out, whatever their size or how well documented they are. Every company still appears in the 3D model and the log.

        How to read it, and what it can't tell you

        Height is how far you can progress. Every ladder starts on the ground floor, L0 Entry level, where everyone starts. Each floor above is a career stage you can reach: you can become Junior, Senior, Lead / Manager, Director or Executive. A ladder's height shows the furthest stage that company offers. For now it's estimated from company size, since bigger companies have more stages: under 100 staff reaches Junior, 100 to 1,000 Senior, 1,000 to 10,000 Lead / Manager, 10,000 to 100,000 Director, and 100,000 or more Executive. A planned next step is to base it on each company's real mix of roles.

        Every floor has a limited number of seats. A company can only have so many directors and executives, so nobody, nepo hires included, can move onto a floor unless a seat there is free. Seats are shared out in the proportions of real US jobs from the BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (May 2025): top executives and general managers about 2.4% of all jobs, other managers 4.8%, supervisors and team leads about 5% (our estimate), professional roles 25.8%, and the rest split evenly between junior and entry-level work. Each company's seats scale with its simulated workforce, and shorter ladders spread the same proportions over fewer floors. A seat frees up when someone is promoted, leaves, retires or is laid off, or when the company grows. When a seat opens, insiders and job seekers compete for it: climbers push about 1.5 times as hard, and nepo hires 2.6 times as hard where their family controls the company (Walmart, by the Walton family; Volkswagen and Porsche, by the Porsche and Piëch families through Porsche SE; Fiat Chrysler and Stellantis, by the Agnelli family through Exor; Dussmann; and, before they collapsed, Schlecker and Arcadia Group) but only 1.4 times elsewhere. A job seeker who finds no free seat on their own floor keeps looking once, then takes a step down. Overall, this keeps about 2% of simulated workers at Executive and about 5% at Director, close to the real shares. A company's career score also shapes its seats: the better the score, the larger the share of its seats from Senior upwards, so the colour changes how far people can get there, not only how fast. That link is our assumption, since no public data gives each company's real mix of senior roles.

        Staying in the same field. About 37 to 39% of US job-to-job moves stay in the same industry sector (Census Bureau Job-to-Job Flows, as analysed by the Richmond Fed for 2019 to 2022). In the model about 40% of job moves stay in the worker's own industry. Some industries are represented by a single company (health care only by HCA Healthcare, government only by the US Postal Service), so that company stands for its whole industry: someone who leaves it can be hired by it again, in place of another employer in the same field. Staying in the field also follows the same rebalancing as hiring, so each industry keeps close to its real share of jobs. Workers passed over for promotion usually look for a better-rated company in their own industry. Career changers always start over in a different industry from the one they left.

        Colour is perception. Glassdoor reviewers choose to post, so scores lean towards people with strong feelings. Scores from a few dozen reviews can swing widely. Large companies mix very different jobs into one number.

        The simulation is a set of rules, not data. Each simulated year, a worker may be promoted. The base chance shrinks as the floors get higher (about 45% a year from Entry level, 35% from Junior, 22% from Senior, 12% from Lead, 5% from Director), and is scaled up or down by the company's career score and cut further if the company is in transition. Ladders appear in the year the company was founded and vanish in the year it collapsed. When a ladder vanishes, about half of its workers find a job on the same floor elsewhere; the rest drop a floor or back to entry level. These numbers are illustrative assumptions chosen to make the dynamics visible.

        Mergers. PSA Group (Peugeot, Citroën, Opel) and Fiat Chrysler merged into Stellantis on 16 January 2021. In the simulation their two ladders slide together and become one double ladder, two ladders glued along a shared middle rail. Workers move over with them: about 65% keep their floor, about 25% are moved down a floor because the merged company has duplicate roles, and about 10% are laid off to the ground. Those shares are illustrative assumptions. The predecessors take Stellantis's colour, since they no longer have reviews of their own.

        The transition rule. A company is marked "in transition" when it has announced major job cuts, a restructuring, a large merger still being integrated, or a review that could lead to selling or closing parts of the business, within the last 12 months. In the simulation, the ladder only fades and slows promotions from the month the transition began: February 2025 for Porsche, when its first job cuts were announced, and July 2026 for FDJ United, when it began reviewing its online business. Before that they behave like any other company.

        Growing ladders. Every company starts small, so ladders grow as companies do. Nvidia's ladder follows its real headcount history: 392 staff in January 2000, past 1,000 in 2002 and past 10,000 in 2017. Companies founded after 2000, such as Airbnb, Spotify, Zalando, Duolingo and MrBeast, appear as a one-floor ladder in their founding year and grow to today's height. Their timing is estimated, assuming fast early growth that slows later. Companies founded before 2000 are shown at today's height throughout, because their headcount history isn't in the model yet.

        The three catastrophes. The model shows 9/11, the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. Their effect on quits and layoffs is calibrated to the whole US economy, using the Bureau of Labor Statistics' JOLTS survey, and applied to every company alike, so the simulated workers behave like the average employee rather than the worst-hit ones. After 9/11, quits fell about 13% while layoffs economy-wide barely moved (−6%): the damage was concentrated in airlines and hotels. In the 2008 crisis, quits fell about 39% and layoffs rose about 20%. In March and April 2020, layoffs jumped about sixfold, with 13.0 million in March alone, and quits fell by a third. Because simulated workers reconsider their jobs only about once a year, this is modelled as one wave of layoffs at lockdown, sized to match. Then came the Great Resignation of 2021–22, when quits rose about 15% above pre-pandemic levels and layoffs fell about 21%. On top of the averages, company-specific shocks still happen as outlier events. Most companies survived all three crises, but some crashed: Sabena after 9/11; Lehman Brothers, Washington Mutual and Circuit City in 2008; Flybe and Arcadia Group during COVID-19, alongside Chrysler's bankruptcy and the airline cuts after 9/11. Their ladders break apart in the model, but everything that happens at failed companies (promotions, quits and the mass layoffs when they collapse) is kept out of the economy chart and the impact table, because extreme cases would distort the picture of the typical worker. The effect on promotions and job-search length is an assumption, because no official data tracks them.

        Industry mix and hiring. Simulated workers are hired so that each industry gets its real share of US jobs (Bureau of Labor Statistics, September 2026): education and health 18%, government 15%, leisure and hospitality 14%, professional and business services 14%, trade 14%, manufacturing 8%, finance 6%, construction 6%, transportation 5%, and information, where most tech companies sit, under 2%. Inside an industry, bigger companies hire proportionally more people, and a better career score makes a company somewhat more attractive. So a tech company can appear in the model as a ladder without taking a bigger share of the workforce than tech really has. Companies' sizes change over time where their growth is modelled.

        Reference employers. To keep the sample representative, the model includes large, typical employers from sectors that were missing: Walmart (retail), HCA Healthcare (health care), JPMorgan Chase (finance), Marriott and Starbucks (hospitality and food service), the US Postal Service (government) and VINCI (construction). Companies best known for one extreme event, such as General Motors' bankruptcy, Citigroup's 52,000 job cuts in 2008 or Boeing's 9/11 layoffs, were left out on purpose.

        Retirement and a settled start. About 2.5% of workers retire each year and are replaced by newcomers at entry level, as in a real workforce. Before the run starts in 2000, the statistical sample quietly lives through 20 years so it begins in a realistic, settled state.

        Quitting and job hunting. Each year a worker may quit and walk their floor looking for something better. The chance follows the company's published attrition rate where there is one: about 13 to 14% at TCS, Infosys, Wipro and Accenture, and 3.7% at Nvidia. Every other company uses its industry's real quit rate from the BLS JOLTS survey, from 0.8% a month in government to 3.4% a month in leisure and hospitality. Layoffs in normal years follow each industry's real layoff rate the same way, from 0.4% a month in government to 2.0% in professional and business services. Job hoppers quit more often, nepo hires rarely. Most people who quit already have their next job lined up: in September 2026 only 741,000 of the unemployed had quit their last job, against 3.1 million quits a month (BLS). So about 9 in 10 simulated quitters move on after a handover of two to six weeks (our estimate from those figures). Everyone else, including everyone laid off, searches for as long as real unemployment lasts (BLS Current Population Survey, September 2026): half find work within about 11.5 weeks, the average is about 25 weeks, and a few take a year or more. During the 2008 crisis and COVID they last about twice as long, as they did in reality. After the search, about half of job seekers land a role one floor higher at another company, which is how many people get promoted in practice. The rest move sideways.

        Why consulting scores so high. Bain, BCG and McKinsey run an "up or out" model: consultants are promoted on a fixed schedule or expected to leave. That makes career progression fast and visible for those who stay, which shows in their scores, but it also means many people exit after a few years.

        Why the shortest ladders are grey. Companies with under 100 staff rarely have enough employee reviews for a reliable score. No well-known company that small, Telegram and Craigslist included, has a usable Glassdoor career rating, so their ladders stay grey rather than being given a guessed colour.

        Missing: the measures that would show stagnation directly, such as time spent at one level before promotion, the share of senior roles filled internally, and mid-level turnover. Most companies don't publish them.

        Release notes

        Version 23 · Same field, real ratings, career score that matters 18 Oct 2026 · current

        • Most careers stay closer to one field. About 9 in 10 job moves used to change industry, about what random picks give. Now about 40% stay in the same industry, close to the 37 to 39% in Census job-to-job data.
        • The career score changes how far you can get. Better-rated companies have a larger share of seats from Senior upwards, so their workers end up higher on average. The economy-wide pyramid stays the same.
        • Two fixes. "Moves to X, which is rated better" now only appears when X really has a higher career score. Career changers now always start over in a different industry; one could restart at the company they had just left.

        Version 22 · Scenarios always reach their event 18 Oct 2026

        • The followed worker lives through the collapse or merger. In "Their company collapses" and "Their company merges", the worker you follow can no longer be laid off before the event, so they are always at Lehman Brothers in September 2008 or at Fiat Chrysler in January 2021. From the moment the event hits them, everything works as normal, and the outcome is where they are in 2026. The other scenarios (ordinary career, high-attrition employer, best-rated employer) have no single event to wait for, so nothing changes there.
        • Followed workers can be hit by the COVID layoff wave. They used to be exempt from it; now they face the same chance as everyone else.

        Version 21 · Real job-search lengths 18 Oct 2026

        • Searches last as long as real ones. Job hunts used to last one to two years, an illustrative guess that left far too many simulated workers out of work. They now follow BLS data on how long unemployment lasts: half end within about 11.5 weeks, the average is about 25 weeks. About 9 in 10 people who quit have the next job lined up and move on within weeks, as BLS figures suggest. Newcomers replacing retirees take a real-length search for their first job. Together this cuts the share of simulated workers out of work from about 40% to about 12%. Quit and layoff rates, seats and the reference sample are unchanged.

        Version 20 · Seats on every floor 18 Oct 2026

        • Nobody gets a seat that doesn't exist. Each floor of each company now has a limited number of seats, in the proportions of real US jobs (BLS, May 2025), so only about 2% of workers can be Executive at once. Promotions, job switches and hires all need a free seat; job seekers who can't find one on their floor eventually step down.
        • Family connections need a family company. Nepo hires start as Senior and climb fast only where their family controls the company, such as Walmart, Volkswagen, Porsche or Stellantis. Elsewhere they start as Junior with a small edge. Climbers now compete harder for open seats than regular employees.
        • The workforce starts as a pyramid. Workers already in place in 2000 are spread over the floors in the same proportions, instead of crowding the upper floors.

        Version 19 · Floors as career potential 18 Oct 2026

        • Floor names describe what you can reach. The floors now read "You can become Junior" up to "You can become Executive", with "Entry level: everyone starts here" on the ground. A ladder's height shows how far you can progress at that company. It's still estimated from company size; basing it on each company's real role structure is the planned next step.

        Version 18 · Surprise scenarios 18 Oct 2026

        • Reveal names the worker type. The panel says which type of worker it was, describes their real start (including a nepo hire's family connections and where the first job was) and how that type behaves, and phrases the ending clearly.
        • Follow one career can keep the scenario secret. "Surprise me" picks one of the five scenarios at random. During the run the journey log stays hidden; when the simulation reaches 2026, a panel reveals which scenario it was and how the career ended, and the full journey unlocks.

        Version 17 · Real turnover by industry 18 Oct 2026

        • Quits and layoffs follow each industry's real rates. The assumed 10% yearly quit chance and 4% layoff chance are replaced by the monthly rates the BLS JOLTS survey measures for each industry. A hotel or restaurant worker now changes jobs about four times as often as a government worker, as in reality. Companies with their own published attrition keep it.
        • Hiring replaces turnover. Industries that lose people faster also hire more, so each keeps its real share of jobs.
        • Industry balance includes retirement. Hiring now replaces everyone who leaves an industry, retirees included, which brought government back to its real share.
        • Balanced starting workforce. In 2000 workers are placed by each industry's real share of jobs, and hiring gently favours any industry that drifts below its real share, as real labour markets rebalance. This fixed government, which had been running about 4 points short.
        • Retirement. About 2.5% of workers retire each year (a roughly 40-year career) and a newcomer starts at entry level, so the workforce doesn't drift upward over 26 years.
        • Warm-up. The statistical sample lives through 20 quiet years before 2000, so the run starts from a settled workforce instead of an artificial starting state.
        • COVID layoff wave. The March–April 2020 layoffs are now applied as one wave at lockdown, sized to the real data, because workers only reconsider their jobs about once a year.
        • Recalibrated: every crisis now matches the direction of the real shift, and 2008 and COVID match its size closely. The crisis shifts still apply on top, and no other features changed.

        Version 16 · A realistic industry mix 18 Oct 2026

Companies that failed, boomed (Amazon, Nvidia, MrBeast) or were hit far harder than average (airlines, hotels, Airbnb, the Stellantis group) are left out of the economy chart and crisis table; they stay in the model.         • Crisis casualties. Added companies that crashed in each crisis: Sabena (9/11), Washington Mutual and Circuit City (2008), Flybe and Arcadia Group (COVID-19). Everything at failed companies is now excluded from the economy chart, not just their final layoffs.

        Version 15 · A robust, representative sample 18 Oct 2026

        • Three catastrophes, calibrated to the whole economy. 9/11, the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic (plus the Great Resignation that followed) now shift quits and layoffs by the same percentages measured across all US employers in the BLS JOLTS survey.         • Baseline layoffs. Workers now face a small, assumed 4% yearly chance of being laid off in normal years, so crisis changes can be measured against a normal level.

        Known limitations

        • No official data tracks promotions or job-search length economy-wide, so their crisis effects are assumptions.
        • The economy-wide data is for the United States; the model includes European and Asian companies.
        • Ladder growth for companies founded before 2000 isn't modelled; they appear at today's height.
        • Short events like the two-month COVID shock give noisier percentages than long ones, even with the large sample.
        • Company stability (credit ratings) is planned for a later version.
